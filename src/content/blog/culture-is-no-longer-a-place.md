You are sitting in your room in India. The ceiling fan is on. Somewhere in the house, someone is watching the news a little louder than they should be. Outside there is traffic, a temple, a neighbour's dog, the particular smell of the street you grew up on. Everything around you is unambiguously here.

You open your laptop.

Within the same hour, you watch a scene from The Sopranos, look at photographs of Japanese architecture, listen to a British band, and read an American teenager's honest thoughts about her parents. You see someone in Copenhagen make coffee in a kitchen that looks nothing like any kitchen you have ever stood in.

Then you close the laptop. You are still in the same room. The fan is still on.

But the room looks slightly different now.

What happens when the culture you are born into is no longer the only culture you know?

## Culture used to be invisible

Nobody wakes up and decides to participate in their culture that day. You just behave.

You dress a certain way. You speak a certain way to your elders and another way to your friends. You know what is embarrassing. You know what is respectable. You know what a marriage is supposed to look like, what children owe their parents, which questions are rude to ask at dinner.

Most of this is learned before you have the machinery to question any of it. That is why it holds. A norm is strongest when it does not feel like a norm. It feels like reality.

For most of human history, this worked because alternatives were invisible. You could disagree with your culture, but you could not easily see past it. The people around you lived inside the same assumptions, and so the assumptions never had to announce themselves. They were just how things were.

## The internet introduced comparison

What the internet really gave us is more lives.

You watch people elsewhere arrange their families differently, fight differently, flirt differently, save differently, grieve differently. You see other definitions of success, other ideas of adulthood, other ways a Tuesday afternoon can look.

And somewhere in all of that watching, a small word enters your thinking.

Before: this is how things are.

After: this is how things are here.

That word, here, creates distance. It turns your culture from the water you swim in into one configuration among many. A choice, even if nobody remembers choosing it.

## The outsider lens, without leaving

An outsider's perspective used to be expensive. You had to leave. Travel, migration, a scholarship, a few years spent being confused in someone else's country. The distance was earned physically, and most people never earned it.

The internet hands you a partial version of that distance for free. You can stay completely embedded in your physical surroundings, same house, same street, same family WhatsApp group, and still develop an enormous mental distance from all of it.

This explains something about our generation's relationship with tradition. It gets misread as enlightenment, or arrogance. The simpler explanation is exposure. We have more opportunities to ask why, because we have already seen that the answer could be different.

Parental authority, marriage timelines, career paths, what counts as respectable work, what a grown person owes their family. Some of these will still be chosen, for good reasons. But they have become visible as choices, and things that are visible as choices get examined in a way that reality never does.

## Shame loses some of its power

Tradition mostly enforces itself through an audience. People will talk. What will relatives say. That is not what people like us do.

But what happens when your audience is no longer the people physically around you?

If everyone in your locality thinks something is strange, you can now find a few million people online who think it is normal, or at least interesting. The internet hands out alternative audiences, and alternative audiences hands out permission for doing things a little differently than how your neighbours would.

This is why someone who grew up on the internet can critique their own surroundings with a bluntness that confuses their elders. They are not standing alone in a hostile room anymore. Psychologically, they have already left it.

## Then something strange happens: we start importing

Here is where the story turns, because the same internet that teaches you to question your culture also teaches you to borrow everyone else's.

Earlier this year, a group of people in Bangalore organised a Bridgerton-themed ball. Gowns, gloves, string arrangements, the whole fantasy. On the internet it was received somewhere between delight and mockery.

It would be easy to make fun of it. I am more interested in what it actually shows.

A person in Karnataka encountered a social ritual through a television show, fell in love with it, and reproduced it thousands of miles from anywhere it ever belonged. The ballroom stopped being British the moment it could be captured, distributed, consumed, admired, and recreated by anyone with a screen and a tailor.

This is what culture does now. It travels.

## Aesthetics travel better than context

The internet is exceptionally good at transmitting surfaces. Clothes, music, interiors, food, dances, fonts, slang. It is much worse at transmitting the conditions that produced those surfaces, the history, the class structure, the century of meaning underneath.

So you can recreate what something looks like far more easily than what it was for. A cultural borrowing can be completely sincere and still feel slightly weightless, because the aesthetic arrived and the context stayed home.

That is not a reason to stop borrowing. It is just worth knowing what is actually in the suitcase.

## Culture becomes modular

Previous generations mostly inherited culture as a bundle. Family, religion, food, clothing, social norms, entertainment. It came as a set, and the set came from one place.

Our generation increasingly picks components. Indian family, Japanese interiors, American individualism, Korean skincare, British music, internet humour. A selection, assembled piece by piece.

Culture becomes something you assemble for yourself.

Which raises a genuinely strange question. If a culture can be assembled from fragments, what does it mean to belong to one?

## The cost of the in-between

It is tempting to make this a celebration. It should not be one.

The outsider lens has a price. If you are always comparing your surroundings with somewhere else, you can end up unable to simply participate in anything. Everything becomes an object of analysis, including your own life. You develop a taste for other places without ever living in them. You become a tourist in every culture, including your own.

You can feel closer to a stranger across the world than to the person next door. You can read the aesthetic language of a foreign city fluently and stumble over the social customs of your own street. You can feel embarrassed by things your parents consider completely ordinary, and completely at home in a subculture you have never physically entered.

You do not fully belong here. You do not belong there either. You are somewhere in between, and the in-between has no festivals.

That might be one of the defining conditions of growing up online. Call it a kind of permanent cultural jet lag.

## Culture as a graph

I do not think the internet is destroying culture. I think it is changing its shape.

For most of history, a culture was a place. Increasingly, it looks more like a network. Someone's cultural identity might be Bangalore crossed with the internet, crossed with their music, their games, their fashion, their communities. Less like a country, more like a graph of connections, some of them physical, most of them not.

I do not know what that produces in the long run. Nobody does. But the unit of culture is shifting from the border to the link, and we are the first people to grow up entirely inside that shift.

## The room again

So. You are sitting in your room. You open your laptop and you are in New Jersey, then Kyoto, then Copenhagen, then Seoul. You close it and you are home again, and the fan is still on, and someone in the house is asking if you have eaten.

Nothing physical moved. But you did.

We are the first generation to grow up simultaneously inside a physical culture and outside it.
